Certificate of Mailing, end to end

The cheapest form of legal proof the Postal Service sells, and the most frequently misapplied. This guide covers what a Certificate of Mailing actually proves, which form provides which level of evidence, which forms are paper only, and how the electronic path works.

Reviewed July 2026 Reading time 7 minutes Level: foundational

What it proves

A Certificate of Mailing is evidence that specific mailpieces were presented to the Postal Service for mailing. It may be presented only at the time of mailing. Each form is postmarked, or round dated, at the moment the mailing is presented, and the postmarked form is returned to the mailer as their receipt.

That receipt establishes one fact with authority: on this date, this sender handed this mailpiece, addressed to this recipient, to the United States Postal Service. For a very large number of legal and regulatory obligations, that is exactly the fact in dispute. Notice requirements are usually written around the date of mailing rather than the date of receipt, which is what makes an inexpensive service into a durable compliance tool.

Why the mailing date matters more than the delivery date

Many statutes, contracts, and regulations start a clock when notice is sent, not when it arrives. Under those rules, proving the mailing date is the entire question, and a recipient who never opened the envelope has no defense. Certificate of Mailing exists to answer precisely that question, at a fraction of the cost of Certified Mail.

What it does not prove

Certificate of Mailing provides no record of delivery. None. The service ends at acceptance. If your requirement is to demonstrate that someone received something, this is the wrong service and you want Certified Mail.

Just as important: the Postal Service does not retain copies of Certificate of Mailing forms. The stamped receipt returned to you is the only evidence that will ever exist. There is no USPS archive to request a duplicate from. If your copy is lost, the proof is gone.

Two consequences worth planning around

First, retention is entirely your organization's responsibility, for the full length of whatever period governs the mailing. Second, a single point of failure in your filing process is a single point of failure in your legal position. Build the archive before you scale the volume.

The forms

Certificate of Mailing is a family of forms, not a single product. PS Form 3817, PS Form 3665, and PS Form 3606-D carry the service itself. PS Form 3877 sits alongside them, sharing the same electronic path without being a Certificate of Mailing form. The table that follows adds the two Certified Mail forms people most often confuse with this family.

PS Form 3817, Certificate of Mailing

For fewer than three individual pieces presented at one time at a retail Post Office, station, or branch. It names the addressee, so it provides per piece evidence. It is a paper form and a counter transaction, it has no electronic version, and it does not scale.

PS Form 3665, Certificate of Mailing — Firm

For three or more pieces listed together. Each sheet is round dated at the time of mailing and returned to the mailer as the receipt. The industry calls it the firm book, and USPS also refers to the individual sheets as firm sheets. Because each listed piece names its addressee, it provides per recipient evidence at volume. This is the form that matters most to compliance operations, and it is the one with an electronic path.

PS Form 3606-D, Certificate of Bulk Mailing — Domestic

Specifies only the number of identical weight pieces mailed. It does not provide evidence that a piece was mailed to any particular address, it provides no record of delivery, and USPS retains no copies. It cannot be used as a certificate of mailing for individual pieces or for itemized lists.

PS Form 3877, Firm Mailing Book for Accountable Mail

The Accountable Mail firm book. USPS describes it as a multipage bound book containing individual sheets with space to enter multiple article numbers for accountable mail such as Certified Mail, COD, Priority Mail Express, and insured pieces. Using the book eliminates the need to generate an individual mailing receipt for each article.

USPS is explicit that PS Form 3877 is not technically a Certificate of Mailing form, and directs mailers listing Certificate of Mailing to use PS Form 3665 or PS Form 3606-D instead. It appears in this guide for two reasons. It shares the same electronic infrastructure, being uploadable via Postal Wizard or Mail.dat. And it is constantly confused with PS Form 3665, because the industry calls both of them the firm book.

PS Form 3877 and PS Form 3665 are not the same thing

Both get called the firm book in conversation. PS Form 3877, Firm Mailing Book for Accountable Mail, lists article numbers for accountable services. PS Form 3665, Certificate of Mailing — Firm, lists pieces for Certificate of Mailing. If someone asks you for "the firm book," ask which service they mean before you answer.

Which forms are paper only

PS Form 3817 and PS Form 3800 have no electronic equivalent. Both are paper documents handled at a retail counter. Automation applies to PS Form 3665, PS Form 3606-D, and PS Form 3877, and to nothing else in this family. Any description of an "electronic 3817" or "electronic 3800" is wrong.

Choosing the right one

Work backward from the obligation rather than forward from the volume. Ask what fact you will need to establish if this mailing is ever challenged.

  • You must show that a named person was notified. PS Form 3665 for volume, PS Form 3817 for one or two pieces. A 3606-D will not do it.
  • You must show a quantity was mailed on a date. PS Form 3606-D is sufficient and cheaper.
  • You must show the notice was received. Certificate of Mailing cannot help. Use Certified Mail, with Return Receipt if a signature is required.
  • You are documenting accountable mail article numbers rather than buying Certificate of Mailing. PS Form 3877 is the form, and it covers Certified Mail, COD, Priority Mail Express, and insured pieces in one book.

Completion rules

These are regulatory requirements, and acceptance clerks enforce them.

  • The mailer completes the form. Entries must be typed, printed in ink or ballpoint pen, or computer generated.
  • Any alteration must be initialed by both the mailer and the acceptance employee.
  • All unused portions of the addressee column must be obliterated with a diagonal line.
  • Forms must show the names and addresses of both sender and addressees, and may show the postage paid.
  • The mailer may add invoice or order numbers to the certificate as a reference, which is a useful hook for tying a receipt back to a case file.

Privately printed forms

USPS permits approved privately printed or computer-generated firm sheets in place of the USPS-supplied PS Form 3665. Three obligations come with that permission, and mailers routinely miss the last two:

  • The mailer must retain the original written approval from USPS as evidence the facsimile was approved. USPS does not keep records of facsimile approvals.
  • The mailer must periodically verify the facsimile against the USPS-provided version and obtain approval of any updated version.
  • A mailer using an approved privately printed form who wants the sheets postmarked must present the forms with the articles at a Post Office facility. Those forms become the mailer's only receipt.

The 50-piece presentment threshold

Certificate of Mailing on PS Form 3665, including approved privately printed versions and the electronic form, with mailings of at least 50 pieces or 50 pounds presented at one time, must be presented to a Post Office business mail entry unit or an authorized detached mail unit. Below that threshold, retail acceptance remains available.

Fees

The Certificate of Mailing fee is charged in addition to regular postage, and it applies to each article on a Form 3817 or listed on a Form 3665, as well as to duplicate copies of either form. When postage evidencing indicia pay the fee, they must show the full numerical value of the amount paid.

Notice 123 prices the service in three structures, and the structure matters more than the headline number: individual pieces are priced per article, firm mailing sheets are priced per piece listed, and bulk quantities are priced per thousand.

Read the first two lines together before choosing a form. At $0.75 per piece listed, a 200-piece firm sheet mailing costs $150.00 in certificate fees and names every addressee. The same 200 pieces on a bulk certificate cost $14.00 and name nobody. That is a real saving and it buys a materially weaker document; the question is not which is cheaper but which one proves what you have to prove.

Two notes on the duplicate line. It is the reason to file the stamped form properly the first time, since USPS retains no copy. And duplicate copies of PS Form 3665 are no longer offered after mailing, though duplicates of PS Form 3817 still are, so for firm sheets the fee line is effectively a request made at the time of mailing rather than a recovery option later.

Prices change on the USPS pricing calendar. Verify against Notice 123 before budgeting, and use the fee in effect on the USPS acceptance date.

The electronic path

Only PS Form 3665, PS Form 3606-D, and PS Form 3877 have an electronic path, and they behave slightly differently:

  • PS Form 3665 may be submitted electronically to PostalOne! and processed at the Business Mail Entry Unit. When submitted electronically, postage must be paid from an Enterprise Payment System account.
  • PS Form 3606-D may be uploaded before the mailing is presented, and receives a watermark date stamped receipt once the mailing is finalized.
  • PS Form 3877 is available for upload via Postal Wizard or Mail.dat, even though it is not itself a Certificate of Mailing form.

Postal Wizard and Mail.dat are the only two submission channels for this family. Mail.XML is a general electronic documentation channel, and it is not a Certificate of Mailing path.

USPS publishes the full procedure in the Automated Certificate of Mailing User Guide. An association explainer is in preparation.

A well designed compliance program usually uses both, matched to what each individual notice type actually has to prove. Sending everything Certified Mail is the most common and most expensive mistake in accountable mail.
