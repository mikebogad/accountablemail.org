Automated Certificate of Mailing

Certificate of Mailing has an electronic path. Most mailers who could be using it are still filling out paper firm sheets by hand. Here is what automation actually replaces, what it does not, and how the process runs from file to round stamped receipt.

What automation means here

A Certificate of Mailing is evidence that you handed specific mailpieces to the Postal Service. It is not evidence that anything was delivered. USPS is explicit about this: Certificate of Mailing provides no record of delivery, and the Postal Service does not retain copies of the forms. The receipt you get back is the only proof that exists, which is exactly why how you obtain and store it matters so much.

Traditionally, that receipt is paper. You complete a form, present it with the mail, a clerk applies a round date stamp, and the stamped form comes back to you. At any volume this becomes a filing operation: printing forms, carrying them to the acceptance unit, waiting for stamping, then scanning and indexing the stamped copies so someone can find one eighteen months later when a claim comes in.

Automation moves the document itself into an electronic submission to PostalOne!, the USPS system that receives electronic documentation from business mailers. You send a file instead of carrying paper. USPS finalizes the mailing and returns a date stamped electronic receipt that you can archive directly into your system of record.

There is a regulatory reason this matters more than it used to. DMM 608.11, effective December 24, 2025, states that a postmark date does not necessarily show when the Postal Service first took possession of a mailpiece, and 608.11.6 states that Intelligent Mail barcode scan data is not evidence of the acceptance date. DMM 608.11.5 names Certificate of Mailing, Certified Mail, and Registered Mail as the services that establish the acceptance date. An organization that needs to prove when it mailed something can no longer point at a postmark or a scan and be done; it needs one of those three services, and it needs to keep the receipt. See DMM 608.11 and what a postmark proves for the full treatment.

The one sentence version

Automated Certificate of Mailing is an option, not a requirement. Where you use it, it replaces the paper form and the round stamp with an electronic submission and a date stamped electronic receipt. The paper forms remain available, and neither path replaces presenting your mail to the Postal Service.

Which forms can go electronic

Certificate of Mailing is not one form. It is a family, and only some members of that family have an electronic path. Getting this distinction right is the single most useful thing on this page.

The 3606-D trap

PS Form 3606-D is the cheapest and easiest certificate to produce, and it is the wrong answer for most compliance use cases. It proves that a quantity of mail existed. It proves nothing about any individual recipient. If your requirement is to demonstrate that you notified a specific person at a specific address on a specific date, a 3606-D will not do it, and you may not discover that until someone challenges it. Use PS Form 3665.

Two submission paths

Automated Certificate of Mailing reaches PostalOne! through two channels, Postal Wizard and Mail.dat, and through no other channel. They are not equivalent in capability, and the right choice depends almost entirely on your volume and whether you have mailing software.

Postal Wizard

Manual entry into the PostalOne! website through the Business Customer Gateway. It supports only simple mailings. It requires no software and no certification, which makes it a reasonable starting point for a low volume operation or a pilot. It does not scale, because someone is typing.

A note on volume thresholds: USPS general eDoc guidance describes Postal Wizard as available for Full-Service mailings under 10,000 pieces. That figure bounds Full-Service eDoc generally. We have not found a USPS document applying it to Certificate of Mailing submission specifically, so treat it as indicative rather than as a stated COM limit, and confirm with your Business Mail Entry contact before planning around it.

Mail.dat

A mailing database, produced by most commercial mail preparation software, that carries all the information about a mailing except the addresses. USPS calls Mail.dat the established industry standard and the optimal solution for most mailings. This is the path serious volume takes. Mail.dat submitters must complete testing in the Test Environment for Mailers before going live in production.

Payment and EPS

Automated Certificate of Mailing requires payment through an Enterprise Payment System account. This applies to PS Form 3665 and to PS Form 3606-D alike; choosing the bulk certificate does not avoid the EPS requirement. It is a hard requirement, not a preference, and it is the item that most often stalls a first implementation, because opening and funding an EPS account is an administrative process that runs on a different clock than the technical work.

Certificate of Mailing fees are charged in addition to regular postage, and Notice 123 prices them in three structures: per article for individual pieces, per piece listed for firm mailing sheets, and per thousand for bulk quantities. Current fees took effect July 12, 2026, and appear in Notice 123, the USPS price list. The full schedule is on the Certificate of Mailing guide. Verify the effective date before modeling cost; these fees change on the USPS pricing calendar, not on yours.

Start the EPS account first

If you are planning an automated COM implementation, open the EPS account before you begin software testing. Technical certification and account setup can run in parallel, and the account is usually the longer pole.

Getting your round stamped proof

The output of the automated process is an electronic, date stamped document rather than a piece of paper with ink on it. For PS Form 3606-D, USPS describes this as a watermark date stamped receipt issued after the mailing is finalized. For Mail.dat submitters, the USPS technical specification documents a process for configuring the PostalOne! Mail.dat client to download the round stamped Certificate of Mailing PDF, and for invoking that download.

Two operational points follow from this, and both are easy to get wrong:

  • Retrieval is a step someone has to own. The document does not push itself into your archive. If nobody has been assigned to download and file it, you will have automated the paperwork and lost the proof, which is worse than where you started.
  • Retention is entirely on you. USPS does not retain copies of Certificate of Mailing forms, and except for Registered Mail and COD items it keeps no mailing records at all for pieces bearing extra services. Whatever your governing statute, regulator, or contract requires, your organization holds the only copy.

Where you present, and what stamps it

This is the most common misunderstanding about automated COM, and it runs in the wrong direction. Automation does not merely fail to eliminate the trip to the Business Mail Entry Unit; it requires one. USPS established the automated option for PS Form 3606-D, PS Form 3665, and PS Form 3877 as a commercial acceptance transaction, processed at a Business Mail Entry Unit or an authorized detached mail unit when the form is electronically uploaded to PostalOne! and paid through EPS. There is no retail path for an electronic submission. A window clerk cannot finalize one, and a mailer who arrives at a counter with an upload will not leave with a receipt.

The paper forms remain accepted everywhere, within the volume thresholds below. Certificate of Mailing may be presented only at the time of mailing, so the mail has to be presented either way. What changes is which door it goes through, and whether a person or a system applies the date.

Paper is the path that works everywhere

If you need proof of mailing and you are not set up for commercial acceptance, the physical forms still work at every acceptance point within the thresholds above. Automation is worth doing, and it is not a prerequisite for getting a round date stamp.

What automation removes is the paper handling around that presentment:

  • Printing and transporting stacks of firm sheets
  • Waiting while a clerk round stamps each sheet by hand
  • Carrying stamped paper back to the plant
  • Scanning, indexing, and physically filing the stamped copies
  • Searching a filing cabinet when someone needs a copy two years later

For a high volume operation, that is the majority of the labor in the process. The trip itself is usually the smallest part of it.

Six common mistakes

  1. Choosing 3606-D because it is simpler. It provides a count, not per recipient evidence. Covered above, and worth repeating because it is the costliest error on this list.
  2. Leaving the EPS account until last. Automated submission of PS Form 3665 and PS Form 3606-D both require it. Start it early.
  3. Skipping certification planning. Mail.dat submitters must pass through the Test Environment for Mailers first. Build the schedule around it.
  4. Nobody owns receipt retrieval. Assign it, monitor it, and alert on missing documents the same way you would alert on a failed job.
  5. Assuming the retention clock is USPS's problem. It is not. USPS keeps no copies.
  6. Treating acceptance practice as uniform. Local practice varies. Talk to your assigned Business Mail Entry contact before your first automated presentment, not after it goes sideways.

How this relates to Certified Mail

Certificate of Mailing and Certified Mail solve different problems, and mailers frequently use both. Certificate of Mailing proves you mailed it. Certified Mail proves you mailed it and produces a delivery record, with Return Receipt adding the recipient's signature.

The connection point is PS Form 3877, the Firm Mailing Book. It lists article numbers for accountable mail including Certified Mail, and it can be uploaded through Postal Wizard or Mail.dat. So an operation automating its Certificate of Mailing workflow is usually touching the same systems, the same EPS account, and the same acceptance relationship it needs for Certified Mail. The two projects should be planned together, not sequentially.

See the Certified Mail guide for the delivery record side of this.
