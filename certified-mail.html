Certified Mail, end to end

The default choice for legal and regulatory notice, and a service most organizations buy more of than they need. This guide covers what Certified Mail includes, what the delivery record actually contains, and where the service reaches its limits.

What the service includes

Certified Mail is an extra service purchased on top of First-Class Mail or Priority Mail. It adds three things to the underlying postage: a unique article number that identifies the piece, a mailing receipt for the sender, and a delivery record that the Postal Service maintains and makes available.

At retail, that mailing receipt is PS Form 3800, Certified Mail Receipt. It is a paper form, it has no electronic version, and it is completed and round dated at the counter. Commercial mailers submitting electronically do not use PS Form 3800 at all; they apply their own barcoded labels carrying the Certified Mail service indicator and document the mailing through PS Form 3877 or electronic documentation instead.

Because it rides on top of a mail class rather than replacing one, the base postage rules still apply. That distinction matters more than it sounds, because it means the choices you make about how the underlying mail is prepared and paid for are separate from the choices you make about the accountable service, and they can be optimized independently.

Certified Mail is a service, not a mail class

You are buying an add-on to First-Class Mail or Priority Mail. Every question about presort, permit, and postage payment is a separate question from the accountable service question, and treating them as one bundled decision is where a lot of avoidable cost hides.

The delivery record

The delivery record is the reason to pay for Certified Mail. USPS records the disposition of the piece and retains that record for a defined period, which means that unlike Certificate of Mailing, there is a Postal Service copy to fall back on.

What the record establishes, in the base service, is that delivery occurred or that delivery was attempted, along with the date. What it does not establish, without additional services, is who signed for it.

Return Receipt, two formats

Return Receipt is a separate extra service that captures the recipient's signature. It is fulfilled in two formats, and choosing between them is one of the more consequential decisions in an accountable mail program. Note that Electronic Return Receipt is not an electronic PS Form 3811. PS Form 3811 is a paper card with no electronic version, and the electronic option is a different fulfillment of the same underlying service.

PS Form 3811, the green card

A physical card attached to the mailpiece. The recipient signs it, and the card travels back through the mail to the sender. The output is a piece of paper with a real signature on it, which is the format most familiar to legal teams and the one most often specified by name in older policies and contracts.

Electronic Return Receipt

The signature is captured at delivery and returned electronically rather than as a physical card. The output is a document, delivered without the round trip through the mailstream.

Check the source of the requirement

Organizations frequently keep using green cards because a policy document written years ago says "return receipt requested" and someone reasonably read that as the physical card. Before changing anything, find the actual governing language, whether it is a statute, a regulation, a contract, or an internal policy that can simply be updated. The answer is often that nothing external requires the card.

Restricted Delivery and signatures

Restricted Delivery limits who may accept the piece, which matters when a statute requires delivery to a named individual rather than to anyone at the address. Adult Signature services impose an age requirement on the person signing. Each is a separate fee, and each is occasionally purchased out of caution rather than requirement. Confirm that a governing rule actually calls for it before adding it at volume, because the per piece cost compounds quickly.

When delivery does not happen

A meaningful percentage of Certified Mail is never delivered, and the reasons carry different legal weight. Your data needs to distinguish between them, and many systems flatten them into a single "not delivered" status, which destroys exactly the information a court would want.

  • Attempted, notice left. USPS tried and left a notice. The recipient may still collect the piece.
  • Refused. The recipient declined to accept it. In many jurisdictions refusal is strong evidence that notice was effective.
  • Unclaimed. The recipient never collected it within the hold period.
  • Undeliverable as addressed. An address quality problem, which is your problem rather than the recipient's, and which usually undermines a notice claim.

The distinction between refused and undeliverable as addressed is often the difference between a defensible notice and a failed one. Preserve it.

Sending Certified Mail at volume

At retail, Certified Mail is a counter transaction with a paper receipt. At volume, it becomes a data operation. Article numbers must be generated and applied, piece data must reach USPS electronically, and scan events must come back into a system where someone can find them.

The mechanics of that electronic exchange, including how accountable mail data reaches USPS and how tracking data returns, are covered in the data and systems track of the knowledge center.

PS Form 3877, the Accountable Mail firm book

PS Form 3877, Firm Mailing Book for Accountable Mail, known in the industry as the Accountable Mail firm book, is a multipage bound book with sheets for entering multiple article numbers for accountable mail, including Certified Mail, COD, Priority Mail Express, and insured pieces. Using the book eliminates the need to generate an individual mailing receipt for each article, which is the whole point of it at volume.

Do not confuse it with PS Form 3665, Certificate of Mailing — Firm. Both are called firm books in conversation, and they are different forms serving different services. PS Form 3877 lists article numbers for accountable services. PS Form 3665 lists pieces for Certificate of Mailing.

USPS is explicit that PS Form 3877 is not technically a Certificate of Mailing form, and that for listing Certificate of Mailing you should use PS Form 3665 or PS Form 3606-D instead. But PS Form 3877 can be uploaded through Postal Wizard or Mail.dat, and through no other channel, which puts it on the same electronic infrastructure as electronically submitted Certificate of Mailing. Operations modernizing one are almost always in a position to modernize the other at the same time.

Getting your data back

Certified Mail generates scan events, and those events are only useful if they reach the system where your business actually works. Two things determine whether they do: your USPS account structure, and your access credentials.

Account structure is the one that quietly breaks. Mailer IDs parented under multiple Customer Registration IDs can produce a situation where a mailer is denied access to data generated by its own mail. The fix is usually re-parenting rather than creating new identifiers, and it is an administrative process worth starting before it becomes urgent.

Where programs go wrong

  1. Buying Certified Mail when Certificate of Mailing would satisfy the requirement. The most expensive habit in accountable mail. Audit your notice types against what each one actually has to prove.
  2. Adding Return Receipt or Restricted Delivery by default. Each is a separate fee and each should be traceable to a governing requirement.
  3. Flattening delivery outcomes. Refused and undeliverable are not the same event. Keep them separate in your data.
  4. Never auditing the underlying postage. The accountable service and the base postage are independent decisions.
  5. Discovering an account structure problem during an audit. Verify that you can actually retrieve your own tracking data before you need to.
