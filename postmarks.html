Home / Knowledge center / Postmarks and postal possession

Regulation  /  DMM 608.11

USPS now says in writing that a postmark does not prove your mailing date

New DMM section 608.11 defines postmarks for the first time in postal regulation. It changes no operations. It changes a great many assumptions, and it names Certificate of Mailing and Certified Mail as the services that actually answer the question.

Reviewed July 2026 Effective December 24, 2025 Level: foundational

What changed

USPS added section 608.11, "Postmarks and Postal Possession," to the Domestic Mail Manual. The standards became effective December 24, 2025, and USPS incorporated them into the January 18, 2026 edition of the online DMM.

USPS is explicit that this changes nothing operationally. Postmarking practices are the same as before. The purpose is to define terms and improve public understanding of postmarks and their relationship to the date of mailing.

Why an association cares about a definition

Because a great many compliance programs rest on an assumption this section quietly contradicts. If your process treats a postmark as evidence of when you mailed something, USPS has now put in regulation that it is not necessarily that. Nothing broke. But the written record of what a postmark means has changed, and written records are what get produced in disputes.

What a postmark is

Under 608.11.1, a postmark is a marking applied by the Postal Service to a mailpiece. What it displays depends entirely on where it was applied:

  • At a retail unit: the name or location of the retail unit, and the date the mailpiece was accepted there.
  • At a processing facility: the name or location of the facility, and the date of the first automated processing operation performed on that mailpiece.

That second definition is the whole issue. A processing facility postmark dates the first automated handling, not the moment USPS took the piece.

Note also that mailpieces prepared according to certain criteria bypass automated cancellation entirely, to improve delivery speed. Those pieces carry no postmark at all.

What a postmark proves

Section 608.11.3 is the part to read twice. Four statements matter:

  1. A postmark confirms USPS accepted custody and that the piece was in USPS possession on the identified date.
  2. The postmark date does not necessarily indicate the first day USPS had possession. A date applied at a processing facility may be later than the date the piece was first accepted.
  3. The absence of a postmark does not imply USPS never took custody, because USPS does not postmark all mail in the ordinary course of operations.
  4. Most postmarks are applied at processing facilities, so they do not necessarily represent either the place or the date of first acceptance.

The practical exposure

A notice mailed on a Friday afternoon may carry a Monday postmark. If a deadline fell on that Friday, and your only evidence is the postmark, your own evidence now argues against you. This was always operationally true. It is now written into the DMM.

Getting a date that matches

Section 608.11.4 tells customers how to obtain a postmark whose date aligns with acceptance.

  • Request a manual (local) postmark at the retail counter. No additional fee, available at any Post Office, station, or branch. Because it is applied on acceptance, the date aligns.
  • PVI labels applied at the counter when a customer simultaneously pays postage and tenders the piece also carry the acceptance date.

There is a volume limit worth knowing. Customers planning to present 50 or more mailpieces for manual (local) postmarks should contact the postmaster or other manager in advance, so adequate resources are available. For any real compliance operation, that threshold makes hand cancellation impractical as a standing process.

Pre-printed postage proves nothing about acceptance

DMM 608.11.4 is blunt about this. Labels applied by the customer before mailing, including postage from self-service kiosks, Click-N-Ship, and meter strips, show only that the customer purchased postage and the date it was printed. They do not demonstrate that USPS accepted the mailpiece, or when. Any workflow that treats a meter date as a mailing date should be reviewed.

What USPS says to use instead

Section 608.11.5 is titled "Services Proving the Date of Postal Acceptance," and it names three:

Services USPS identifies as proof of acceptance date
ServiceDMM referenceWhat it provides
Certificate of Mailing503.5.0Evidence that individual mailpieces were presented for mailing
Certified Mail503.3.0A mailing receipt for individual pieces, plus a delivery record
Registered Mail503.2.0A mailing receipt for individual pieces, plus chain of custody

This is the clearest statement USPS has published on why these services exist. The postmark is not the instrument for proving a mailing date. These three are, and USPS now says so in the regulation itself.

Scans and tags are not proof

Section 608.11.6 addresses auxiliary markings and data, and this part surprises technically sophisticated mailers most.

USPS states that auxiliary markings and scan data indicate possession of a mailpiece, but do not constitute evidence of the date when USPS first accepted possession. Their absence likewise implies nothing. The section names two examples:

  • Fluorescent identification tags, imprinted on pieces processed on automated machines, encoding among other things the date the tag was applied.
  • Intelligent Mail barcode scans. USPS is direct here: the IMb does not verify Postal Service possession, because the customer applies it before tendering the piece. Scans reflect the time and place of each scan, nothing more.

USPS also notes that where a mailer includes unique serial numbers, IMb scan data can track specific pieces, and that commercial mailers access that data through Informed Visibility. It adds a warning that duplicate or illegible barcodes compromise the availability and reliability of scan event data.

Tracking data is operational, not evidentiary

Scan data is genuinely valuable for operations, service measurement, and knowing what happened to a piece. What it is not, per USPS, is proof of when the Postal Service first took possession. If your compliance evidence rests on an IMb scan, this section is a problem for you, and the remedy is a mailing receipt.

What to do about it

  1. Find out what your process actually relies on. For each notice type, ask what document you would produce to prove the mailing date. If the answer is a postmark, a meter date, or a scan, that is a gap.
  2. Check your policy language. Internal procedures written years ago often say "postmarked by" when the drafter meant "mailed by." Those are now demonstrably different things.
  3. Match the service to the requirement. Where the mailing date governs and receipt does not, Certificate of Mailing is the cheaper correct answer. Where receipt must be shown, Certified Mail.
  4. Do not plan around hand cancellation at volume. The 50-piece advance notice threshold makes it a retail accommodation, not a production process.
  5. Tell your legal team. This is a regulatory definition change with evidentiary consequences, and they will want to know before opposing counsel does.
