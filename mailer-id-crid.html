Data, systems, and compliance

Mailer IDs, CRIDs, and why your tracking data does not arrive

Account structure is the least interesting subject in accountable mail and the one most likely to break your compliance reporting. Since April 2026, it also determines whether you can retrieve tracking data for your own mail at all.

Why this matters now

For years, a messy USPS account structure was an annoyance. Reports came from several places, somebody stitched them together in a spreadsheet, and the organization moved on.

That changed in April 2026. USPS now ties tracking data access to the Mailer ID embedded in the barcode and to whether the requesting party has an authorized relationship to that MID. Possession of a tracking number is no longer sufficient. If your account structure does not clearly establish that you own or are authorized for the MID on your own mail, your requests can fail.

This is the failure mode that surprises people

An organization can be denied access to scan data generated by mail it paid for and sent, because the MID on that mail is parented under a different CRID than the account making the request. Nothing is broken, no rule was violated, and the fix is administrative. But it is usually discovered during an audit, at the worst possible moment.

What a CRID is

A Customer Registration Identification number is a USPS-generated numeric code of up to 15 digits that uniquely identifies a business at a location. USPS creates one for you automatically when you create a Business Customer Gateway account, and you can see it on your account page.

The CRID is the anchor. It connects a business location's information across USPS applications, and it associates every permit and every Mailer ID issued to that location. When you submit electronic documentation, a CRID is required, because it identifies the business entity doing the submitting.

What a Mailer ID is

A Mailer ID is a field inside the Intelligent Mail barcode that identifies the mailer. USPS assigns MIDs to mail owners, mailing agents, and other service providers who request them.

MIDs are either 6 digits or 9 digits. The length is a function of volume: USPS assigns MIDs based on annual mail volume as verified by volume reported in PostalOne!. Shorter is higher volume, which is the opposite of what most people guess the first time.

How the two relate

The rule that governs everything downstream is short: each MID is owned by a single CRID at any given time, and each mailing permit is owned by a single CRID at any given time. A single CRID may own many MIDs and many permits. A MID may not be shared across CRIDs.

That one-directional ownership is why structure decides access. Permissions, data distribution profiles, and reporting all resolve through the CRID that owns the MID.

How many you can have

Every business location gets one 9-digit MID without needing to demonstrate any volume. Beyond that, additional MIDs are earned through volume.

Where fragmentation comes from

Nobody designs a fragmented structure. It accumulates. The usual causes:

  • Multiple registrations over time. Different people at different sites each created a Business Customer Gateway account, generating a CRID apiece.
  • Acquisitions. An acquired entity brings its own CRIDs, MIDs, and permits, and nobody consolidates them because nothing appears broken.
  • Service provider arrangements. A provider obtained MIDs on your behalf, parented somewhere you did not expect.
  • Product line separation. Someone deliberately separated identifiers to keep reporting clean, without realizing it would also separate data access.
  • Address changes. A CRID identifies a business at a location. Move the location, and you can end up with a new one.

The April 2026 access control change

Beginning April 1, 2026, USPS applied new access control requirements to Tracking APIs, webhook subscriptions, and scan event extracts. The stated purpose is to improve data security, protect mailer information, and ensure that only authorized and properly registered users can reach tracking data. USPS also retired the legacy Web Tools tracking platform.

What the access rules require
ChannelRequirement
Tracking APIYou must be authorized to access the Mailer ID included in the package barcode or the tracking number itself.
Tracking WebhookSame authorization requirement as the Tracking API.
Scan Event ExtractThe Mailer ID owner must delegate access to you.

The good news for most mailers: if you ship directly, create your own labels, and the MID embedded in the barcode is registered to you, USPS continues to provide tracking data at no cost through the Tracking API, Tracking Webhook, or Scan Event Extract. The requirement is that the registration actually reflects reality, which is exactly what a fragmented structure undermines.

Organizations that need to establish or repair access generally work through some combination of the following. Confirm the current list with USPS before starting, since the program is still settling:

  • Submitting an inquiry to USPS
  • Establishing an Enterprise Payment System account
  • Signing an order form and intellectual property agreement
  • Configuring USPS Business Portal account permissions
  • Reviewing and accepting the updated terms and conditions in the Business Portal, under My Apps, Developer Apps, Manage

Verify before you need it

Make a test data request against a MID from each of your mailing streams, today. A denial in a test is an administrative task. The same denial during a regulatory examination is a finding.

Delegation and data distribution

Two mechanisms let data reach the right party without moving identifiers around.

MID delegation lets a mail owner delegate management of its Mailer ID to a mailing agent, so the agent can manage that MID with USPS on the owner's behalf.

Data distribution profiles govern who receives scan data for services including Full-Service Address Change Service, IMb Tracing, Full-Service Start-The-Clock and container visibility, tray visibility, and bundle visibility. A profile can be delegated so that it is managed from one account or by someone else.

One prerequisite catches people out: a mail owner is eligible to perform MID delegation or data distribution for Full-Service Address Change Service information only if it holds a Mailer ID as well as a CRID. A CRID alone is not enough.

Diagnosing your own structure

This is a half-day exercise for most organizations, and it is worth scheduling before it becomes urgent.

  1. Inventory your CRIDs. Log in to the Business Customer Gateway and list every business location and CRID on the account profile. Then ask whether other people in the organization have their own Gateway accounts, because they probably do.
  2. Inventory your MIDs. Use the Mailer ID tool to list every MID and note which CRID owns each one.
  3. Map MIDs to mail streams. For each production job that sends accountable mail, identify which MID appears in the barcode.
  4. Find the orphans. Flag any mail stream whose MID is owned by a CRID other than the one your reporting and data access run through. Those are your exposures.
  5. Test each one. Attempt an actual data retrieval per stream and record what happens.

Fixing it

When a MID sits under the wrong CRID, the instinct is to request a new MID under the right one. That is usually the wrong move. A new MID applies only to mail produced after it is put into service, so it does nothing for the historical data you cannot currently reach, and it adds another identifier to an inventory that is already too large.

Re-parenting the existing MID to the correct CRID is generally the better path. It preserves the association with mail already in the stream, it reduces rather than increases the number of identifiers, and it fixes the historical access problem rather than working around it.

Where to start

Consolidation requests go through USPS rather than being self-service. Your Business Mail Entry contact and the PostalOne! Customer Care Center are the usual entry points. Bring the full inventory from the diagnostic above with you, because the first question will be which identifiers should end up where.

One more point of discipline: decide who owns account structure inside your organization and write it down. In most operations nobody does, which is precisely how the structure drifted in the first place.
